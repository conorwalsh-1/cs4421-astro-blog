Alexander Scriabin (1872–1915) studied at the Moscow Conservatory alongside Rachmaninoff, but the two ended up in very different places. Scriabin's music changes so much over twenty-five years that it helps to hear it in three stages.

## 1. The Romantic (c. 1886–1903)

The early piano music grows directly out of **Chopin**: preludes, mazurkas, nocturnes and études. Start with the **Étude in D♯ minor, Op. 8 No. 12** (1894). Its leaping left hand and octave melody show a young virtuoso pianist writing for his own hands.

*Listen next:* Prelude Op. 11 No. 1, Étude Op. 2 No. 1, Sonata No. 2 "Sonata-Fantasy" Op. 19.

## 2. The ecstatic middle (c. 1903–1910)

Harmony loosens, rhythms get restless, and the music starts to reach for something beyond itself. The **Poem of Ecstasy** for orchestra and the single-movement **Sonata No. 5, Op. 53** (1907) belong here. They are full of trills, fanfares and sudden flights.

*Listen next:* Sonata No. 4 Op. 30, *Poème satanique* Op. 36.

## 3. The mystic (c. 1910–1915)

After *Prometheus*, keys disappear. The late sonatas (Nos. 6–10) are built from synthetic chords like the [Mystic chord](/blog/the-mystic-chord/). The Seventh was nicknamed the "White Mass" and the Ninth the "Black Mass". The short poem **Vers la flamme**, Op. 72 is the best place to hear this style for the first time.

## The Mysterium

Scriabin planned a final work, the *Mysterium*, to be performed over seven days in the foothills of the Himalayas, combining music, light, scent and dance. He died in April 1915 before he got past sketches for its prologue, the *Prefatory Action*. The composer Alexander Nemtin later spent decades realising those sketches.
