Listen to the opening of *Prometheus* or any of the late sonatas and one sound keeps coming back: a shimmering, unresolved six-note chord. On C it's **C – F♯ – B♭ – E – A – D**. Scriabin's contemporaries called it the *Mystic* or *Prometheus* chord.

Press **Play the Mystic chord** above. The chord rolls up from C, and the light panel shows each note's colour.

## Built from fourths

Stack the notes and look at the gaps. Almost every interval is a fourth: an augmented fourth (C–F♯), then diminished, augmented and perfect fourths. A chord built in fourths has no obvious "root" the way a triad does. That's why it floats instead of resolving.

It also sits very close to the upper harmonics of the overtone series on C, which helps explain why it sounds rich and not harsh.

## A whole world from one chord

In the late works Scriabin uses chords like this one less as a chord and more as a **pitch collection**. Melody and harmony both come from it. Spread the same notes inside one octave and you get the **Prometheus scale** (C D E F♯ A B♭), which the second button plays.

## Practising it

- Voice the chord with the fourths spread across both hands: left hand C–F♯–B♭, right hand E–A–D.
- Pedal generously but change often. The colour comes from resonance, not volume.
- Try moving the chord up in minor thirds. The colours on the keyboard change, but the sound keeps its strange, weightless quality.
