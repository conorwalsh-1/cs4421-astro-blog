In 1910 Alexander Scriabin finished *Prometheus: The Poem of Fire*, Op. 60, for orchestra, piano, organ and choir. At the top of the score is a line marked **Luce**, meaning "light". It is written in ordinary notation, but it isn't meant to be heard. It was meant to be *seen*, played on a *clavier à lumières* (a "keyboard of lights") that would flood the hall with colour.

## A colour for every key

Scriabin linked each key with a colour. C was red, D yellow, A green, and F♯ a bright, saturated blue. Arrange those keys around the **circle of fifths** (C, G, D, A, E, B, F♯…) and the colours move roughly in spectral order: red, orange, yellow, green, then the blues and violets. That is too neat to be accidental. Many scholars think his "synaesthesia" was at least partly an intellectual system and not a purely involuntary perception. Even so, he spoke about the colours with total conviction.

| Key | Colour |
| --- | ------ |
| C   | red |
| G   | orange-pink |
| D   | yellow |
| A   | green |
| E   | pearly white-blue |
| B   | pale blue |
| F♯  | bright blue |
| D♭  | violet |
| A♭  | purple-violet |
| E♭  | flesh, with a steely sheen |
| B♭  | steel, with a metallic sheen |
| F   | deep red |

## The light that failed

The 1911 Moscow premiere went ahead *without* the light part, because the instrument wasn't ready. The first performance with coloured light came in New York's Carnegie Hall in March 1915, only weeks before Scriabin's death. By most accounts the projection was underwhelming. The idea was a century ahead of the technology.
