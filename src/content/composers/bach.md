Bach spent his whole life as a working musician: church organist, court musician at Köthen, and from 1723 until his death the Thomaskantor in Leipzig, responsible for the music of the city's main churches. Most of his keyboard music was written for teaching, for worship, or for his own playing.

**The Well-Tempered Clavier** is the cornerstone. Each of its two books contains 24 preludes and fugues, one in every major and minor key. It showed that a keyboard tuned so that *every* key was usable could explore the whole tonal world, and it has been a pianist's daily bread ever since.

Bach knew the earliest pianos. According to his pupil Johann Friedrich Agricola, he criticised one of Gottfried Silbermann's early instruments for its heavy action and weak treble, and later approved of Silbermann's improved models. In 1747, visiting Frederick the Great at Potsdam, he improvised on the king's fortepianos; the theme the king gave him became *The Musical Offering*.

On a modern piano, Bach's lines can be shaped with dynamics his harpsichord never had. Glenn Gould's 1955 recording of the Goldberg Variations made that approach famous.
